How to Calculate GST in Australia: Formulas, Worked Examples and a Quick-Reference Table

IG
The Invoice-Generator.com.au team 16 October 2026 · 10 min read

How to calculate GST in Australia comes down to three moves: multiply a GST-exclusive price by 1.1 to add GST, divide a GST-inclusive price by 11 to find the GST inside it, and divide by 1.1 to get the price before GST. Below are the formulas, a quick-reference table, worked examples for mixed baskets and target prices, spreadsheet formulas and the mistakes that put the wrong figure on an invoice. If you just want the answer for one number, the GST calculator does both directions instantly.

This page is the maths. Whether you should charge GST at all (registration, GST-free items, overseas clients) is covered in do I add GST to my invoice?

What are the GST formulas in Australia?

GST is 10% of the GST-exclusive price. That one fact gives you every formula: the GST-inclusive price is 110% of the exclusive price, so the GST is 10/110, or one-eleventh, of the inclusive price.

Before you calculate anything, decide which column your number belongs in. "$528" on a supplier's receipt is almost always GST-inclusive. "$480 + GST" on a quote is exclusive. Most wrong GST figures start with the wrong starting point, not bad arithmetic.

How do I add GST to a price?

Multiply the GST-exclusive price by 1.1. If you need the GST as its own line on a tax invoice, multiply by 0.1 instead and add it to the subtotal. A $95 hourly rate becomes $95 + $9.50 GST = $104.50.

On an invoice, work out GST on the subtotal of taxable items, not on each hour or each unit, unless your software does it per line. Eight hours at $95 is $760, GST is $76.00, and the total is $836.00. The same number comes out either way when every line is a whole number of cents, but per-line rounding can drift by a cent on odd amounts (see rounding below).

How do I work out the GST component of a GST-inclusive price?

Divide the GST-inclusive price by 11. That's the GST component the ATO expects to see on a tax invoice, and the figure you claim as a GST credit when you buy. A $2,640 inclusive price contains $2,640 ÷ 11 = $240 of GST.

To get the price before GST, either divide by 1.1 ($2,640 ÷ 1.1 = $2,400) or subtract the GST from the total ($2,640 − $240 = $2,400). Subtracting is safer once rounding is involved, because the GST and the ex-GST figure then always add back to the exact total.

Why divide by 11 and not multiply by 0.9 or take off 10%?

Because the 10% was calculated on the smaller, pre-GST price. Taking 10% off the bigger, GST-inclusive price removes too much. On a $1,100 invoice the GST is $100, not $110, and the price before GST is $1,000, not $990.

A $10 error on one invoice looks small. Across a quarter of supplier receipts it means you're claiming GST credits you aren't entitled to, and your BAS won't match your tax invoices.

How do I calculate GST on an invoice with GST-free items?

Calculate GST only on the taxable lines, never on the whole total. Add up the taxable items, take 10% (or divide the inclusive figure by 11), and leave GST-free lines out of the GST calculation. The ATO says a tax invoice that mixes taxable and non-taxable items must clearly show which items are taxable, the GST and the total.

GST-exclusive example. A wholesaler supplies a café with fresh produce ($320.00, GST-free), cleaning products ($80.00, taxable) and paper cups ($45.00, taxable).

  • Taxable subtotal: $80.00 + $45.00 = $125.00
  • GST: $125.00 × 0.1 = $12.50
  • Invoice total: $320.00 + $125.00 + $12.50 = $457.50

Taking 10% of the full $445.00 would have charged $44.50 of GST, $32.00 too much.

GST-inclusive example (working out a receipt). A supermarket receipt totals $86.40, of which $52.80 is taxable items and $33.60 is GST-free food. The GST is $52.80 ÷ 11 = $4.80. Dividing the whole $86.40 by 11 gives $7.85, which would overclaim $3.05 in GST credits. Many receipts mark the taxable items or print the GST total at the bottom; use that figure rather than recalculating.

How do I round GST?

Round GST to the nearest cent, with half a cent rounding up. With one taxable item that's the whole rule. With several taxable items the ATO allows two methods, which can differ by a cent, and you and your customer don't have to use the same one.

  • Total invoice rule: work out the GST on each taxable item without rounding, add them, then round the total. Items of $2.45, $3.65 and $1.25 give $0.245 + $0.365 + $0.125 = $0.735, which rounds to $0.74.
  • Taxable supply rule: work out the GST on each item, rounding each one where your system can't hold the extra decimals, then add them. Rounded per line, the same items give $0.25 + $0.37 + $0.13 = $0.75.

Both come from the ATO's rounding of GST guidance. Pick one and use it on every invoice. On normal invoice amounts the difference is a cent or two, and it only shows up when lines have half-cent GST.

How do I work backwards from a GST-inclusive target price?

Divide the target by 1.1 to get the ex-GST price, but don't then add 10% back on and expect the target. Rounding can push the total a cent over. The cleaner fix is to enter the target as a GST-inclusive price and let the GST be one-eleventh of it.

Say you want a customer to pay exactly $500. $500 ÷ 1.1 = $454.5454…, which rounds to $454.55. Add 10%: $45.455 rounds to $45.46, and the total is $500.01. Enter $500 as GST-inclusive instead and the invoice shows GST of $500 ÷ 11 = $45.45, ex-GST $454.55, total $500.00.

The opposite target works the other way. If you need $500 in your pocket before GST, charge $500 + $50 GST = $550. Quoting a round $500 "including GST" leaves you $454.55. The free Australian GST calculator handles both: switch between add and remove GST and the figures update as you type.

The same formulas work in Excel and Google Sheets. Wrap GST in ROUND so the cents on your spreadsheet match the cents on your invoice, and get the ex-GST figure by subtraction so the columns always add up.

How do I show the GST on a tax invoice?

Show the GST as its own line under the subtotal, or, if every item is taxable and the GST is exactly one-eleventh of the total, write "Total price includes GST". The ATO accepts either on a tax invoice. Once there's a GST-free item on the invoice, show the GST as a figure and mark which lines are taxable.

Common GST calculation mistakes

  1. Taking 10% off an inclusive price. $1,100 includes $100 of GST, not $110. Divide by 11.
  2. GST on GST-free lines. Most basic food, exports and some health and education services carry no GST. Calculate on the taxable subtotal only.
  3. Charging GST when you're not registered. The calculation is right but the GST shouldn't be there. Check the GST registration threshold first.
  4. Adding GST to a price that already includes it. A supplier's $220 inclusive receipt doesn't become $242. If the source says "inc GST", divide; if it says "+GST" or "ex GST", multiply.
  5. Back-solving a round inclusive price and getting $500.01. Enter the target as GST-inclusive rather than adding 10% to a rounded ex-GST figure.
  6. Rounding each line one way and the total another. Pick the total invoice rule or the taxable supply rule and stick to it.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate GST in Australia?

To add GST, multiply the GST-exclusive price by 1.1 (or by 0.1 for the GST amount alone). To find the GST inside a GST-inclusive price, divide it by 11. To get the price before GST, divide the inclusive price by 1.1 or subtract the GST from it.

How do I work out the GST component of a price?

Divide the GST-inclusive price by 11. A $2,640 price includes $240 of GST, and the price before GST is $2,640 − $240 = $2,400.

Why can't I just take 10% off the GST-inclusive price?

Because GST is 10% of the smaller pre-GST price. Taking 10% off a $1,100 inclusive price gives $110 of GST and $990 ex-GST, when the correct figures are $100 and $1,000.

How do I calculate GST when some items are GST-free?

Calculate GST only on the taxable items. A $457.50 invoice with $320 of GST-free produce and $125 of taxable goods has GST of $12.50, not 10% of the whole $445.

How do I round GST?

Round to the nearest cent, with half a cent rounding up. With several taxable items the ATO allows the total invoice rule or the taxable supply rule, which can differ by a cent. Use one method consistently.

What is the Excel formula for GST?

For the GST to add to an ex-GST price in A2, use =ROUND(A2*0.1,2). For the GST inside an inclusive price, use =ROUND(A2/11,2). For the price before GST, use =A2-ROUND(A2/11,2).
